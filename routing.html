What a perfect router could have bought

Voting is one way to combine cheap models; routing is the other: look at the question first, then send it to one model chosen for that kind of question. This page asks how much routing could possibly gain in the same ten model markets as the hard-core page, four historical and six live, by giving the router something no real router has: hindsight.

October 2026 · from the efficient-AI-markets project · benchmark: MMLU

The question, and the rules of the game

Fix a market: the models on sale on one date, at that day's prices, and their recorded answers to the same questions of MMLU, a four-option multiple-choice benchmark across 57 school and professional subjects. The four historical markets use the HELM leaderboard's recorded answers to all 14,038 test questions; the six live markets, July 2024 to September 2026, use our own API calls on a fixed 2,000-question draw. Fix a budget. The frontier is the most accurate single model you can afford, and its error is the number to beat. We use the per-query cap throughout: every call must cost at most the budget. (The other convention, where only the average bill is capped, lets a buyer split traffic between a cheap and a dear model; this page does not use it.)

A router splits the questions by a label it can read for free before paying anything, and sends each group to one affordable model. We give the router three labels of increasing fineness, and we let it choose, for every group, the model that turned out best there. That is why we call it an oracle: it is chosen in hindsight, on the same questions it is scored on. Every gap on this page is therefore a ceiling on what routing could gain, not a result a buyer could have realised. The three labels:

  1. The four super-categories of MMLU (STEM, humanities, social sciences, other), the grouping from the benchmark's own repository.
  2. The 57 subjects (college mathematics, virology, professional law, and so on), the label a deployed router could read from the exam section header.
  3. The question itself: a separate choice for every single question. No router can do this; it is the upper envelope of anything that picks one model per question, and it mostly measures how few questions defeat every affordable model at once.

Two honesty notes, before the chart. First, nothing here is cross-fitted: a real router trained on some questions and scored on others it had not seen would do worse, and the one careful study of that on this benchmark (Meena's, September 2026, on the cleaned MMLU-Redux subset with 57 models) found that training and luck controls cut the subject-level gain to about half a point to one point. Second, the standard control for routing, rerunning everything on random groups of the same sizes, is deliberately not on this page yet; until it is, the fine-grained numbers should be read as part specialization, part luck.

How to read what the router does

The sidebar's subject table is the interesting part: it is legible specialization. Where the gap is real, the assignments make sense on their face: a mathematics-leaning model takes the quantitative subjects, a careful instruction-follower takes law and ethics, and a cheap model matches the frontier on factual-recall subjects at a fraction of the price. Where the gap is luck, the table shows a long tail of models each "winning" one or two subjects by a question or two, the signature of selection on noise. The one study with proper controls on this benchmark found mostly the second kind beyond a point of genuine gain.

The per-question oracle line answers a different question: how often is some affordable model right? Its distance below the frontier (fifteen to twenty-five points in these markets) is sometimes read as the promise of routing. The subject line shows how little of that promise survives even a perfect router confined to labels a buyer actually has, and the hard-core page shows why: the models' errors concentrate on a shared set of questions, so splitting traffic by any cheap label finds little complementary skill to harvest.

Provenance

Historical answers: the HELM leaderboard's recorded per-question predictions (MMLU, 5-shot, letter-only format), 14,038 questions after dropping items with two marked answers, at list prices in force on the market date, per 1,000 questions at the audit's nominal token counts; open-weight models at a host's 2024 size tiers. Markets: October 2023 (8 models), April 2024 (31), July 2024 (53), January 2025 (77). Live answers: our own API calls on a fixed 2,000-question MMLU draw, temperature 0, one direct prompt, with costs measured from each call's own token counts at the prices in force on the snapshot date. Live markets: July 2024 (5 models), January 2025 (16), July 2025 (30), January 2026 (45), July 2026 (60), September 2026 (62). The oracle computation is eamh/src/build_routing_widget.py in the project repository, cross-checked against an independent implementation (eamh/src/routing_oracle_helm.py); the voting and hard-core results for the same markets are on the hard-core page.
