What a perfect verifier could have bought

Voting fails because the wrong majority drowns a lone correct answer. This page asks the opposite extreme: suppose a buyer could check answers perfectly, and only had to get one right. How much would that have been worth, in the same model markets as the rest of this site?

October 2026 · from the efficient-AI-markets project · benchmark: MMLU

The question, and the rules of the game

Fix a market: the models on sale on one date, at that day's prices, and their recorded answers to MMLU, a four-option multiple-choice benchmark (all 14,038 questions in the four historical markets; our own 2,000-question collection in the six live ones). Fix a budget. The frontier is the most accurate single model you can afford, and its error is the number to beat.

The buyer picks a team of models once, pays for every member on every question, so the team's combined price per question must fit the budget, and then a perfect verifier reads the submitted answers and returns a correct one whenever any team member produced one. The verifier is free and never wrong. The team errs exactly on the questions where every member is wrong at once. Finding the best affordable team is a classic optimization problem (budgeted maximum coverage), and unlike voting weights it can be solved exactly: every budget on this page carries a proof of optimality, from two independent solvers that agree everywhere.

Three honesty notes. First, a free, perfect verifier is not for sale, so nothing here is an arbitrage a buyer could execute; it is the exact ceiling for every scheme that generates several answers and selects one, and a real verifier can only do worse, by its own mistakes and its price. Second, unlike the routing page's ceilings, these numbers survive the honesty test: teams chosen on two thirds of the questions and scored on the unseen third lose at most a fifth of a point, so the gaps are structure, not selection luck; the held-out number is shown beside every budget. Third, on four-option multiple choice a scheme allowed to submit all four constant answers would be perfect by construction; only answers the models actually submitted count.

Two patterns. The gaps are an order of magnitude above anything voting achieved in the same markets, because each model keeps mistakes of its own that a checker can harvest and a vote cannot reach. And the ceiling is falling as the menus fill in: the share of questions that every model on sale misses at once fell from 5.3% to 0.6% across the historical markets, and from 6.8% to 1.3% across the live ones.

What to make of it

This is the hard-core page's finding seen from the other side. Models share one ladder of difficulty, so voting among them buys almost nothing; but each keeps idiosyncratic errors, and a perfect checker harvests exactly those. At the top budget the team is the whole menu and its error is precisely the set of questions every model on sale misses at once, the violet set of the hard-core page. That set, and with it the whole ceiling, has shrunk every year: whatever verification will eventually cost, the market is already pricing away the thing it would buy.
