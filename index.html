The questions every affordable AI model fails together

If the market for AI models is efficient, the cheap models must share one common set of hopeless questions: a hard core. This page computes that set exactly, in ten model markets from 2023 to 2026, lets you click through every market and budget to see what the computation found, and shows what the same test does to a market deliberately built to violate the theory.

September 2026 · from the efficient-AI-markets project · benchmark: MMLU

The prediction being tested

Language models are sold as API calls at prices spanning two orders of magnitude, and the expensive ones are usually more accurate. The efficient market hypothesis for AI says that in a competitive market, no cheap trick that combines the models already for sale should beat the best single model you could buy for the same money; if one existed, someone would sell it, and the advantage would disappear into the price list.

That hypothesis has a sharp, testable consequence. Take any collection of models a buyer could call together within a budget. If no fixed combination of them beats the best single affordable model, then they must share a hard core: one set of questions, covering at least twice the best model's error rate, on which every model in the collection does no better than a coin flip, even after the best fixed relabeling of its answers. Not merely "they all score poorly": any reliable edge on those questions, in either direction, is something a weighted vote could harvest. Five 90-percent models with no vote that beats them must all be right on the same 80 percent of the test. The size and existence of that core is computable from the models' recorded answers, which is what this page does.

How each line was computed, step by step

Everything on this page comes from one procedure, repeated per market and per budget. In the charts below, the horizontal axis is the buyer's budget, and each line is drawn in the margin beside the step that computes it. Here is exactly what was done.

  1. Rebuild the market as it stood on the date. Take every model released and on sale at the snapshot date, at that date's list prices, and compute its cost per query. Take each model's recorded answer to every question: 14,038 MMLU questions from HELM's public per-question predictions for the four historical markets, and 250 MMLU questions from our own API calls for the six live markets. An answer is one of the letters A to D, or "no parse" when the model's output contained no readable letter. We keep "no parse" as a fifth output symbol rather than discarding it; that choice matters later.
  2. The black line: twice the frontier error. Lay a grid of budgets over the market's price range. At each budget B, the frontier is the most accurate single model whose per-query price is at most B; call its error rate α(B). The black line plots 2α(B). It is doubled because that is the size the theorem demands of the core: if no combination beats the frontier, a hard core of size at least 2α(B) must exist. (Why twice: the core must hold each model at fifty percent, so about half of it falls on questions the model gets right, and only the other half on its α of errors.)
  3. ▲▲Pick the collection of models to test. At each budget we form the near-frontier affordable collection: every model whose error is within one point of the best model at or below its own price (so, models that are honest value at their price, not junk), added cheapest first for as long as the collection's combined price still fits within B. This is a set of models one buyer could call together on the same query, for at most B. When you select a budget in the widget, these are the triangles, each drawn at its own price and twice its error.
  4. The light-blue line: the largest hard core β of that collection. Now the actual measurement. We look for a weighting of the benchmark: each question i gets a weight wi between 0 and 1 (so no question can be counted more than once, which forces the core to be spread out rather than piled on a few freak questions). The weighting must satisfy, for every model in the collection and for every fixed relabeling of its five output symbols into answer letters (all 45 = 1,024 maps per model, such as "read C as B, read no-parse as A"): under these weights, the model answers at most half of the weight correctly. The same must hold for each constant guess, so no answer letter may carry more than half the total weight. Among all weightings satisfying this, a linear program finds one of maximum total weight, exactly. That maximum, as a share of the benchmark, is β, the light-blue line. A larger total weight also means a more spread-out core once normalised, so "largest" and "smoothest" are the same thing here. In practice the optimal weights come out almost entirely 0 or 1, so the witness is literally a list of questions; the green dotted line repeats the computation restricted to exact 0/1 weights (built by rounding the weighting and repairing any violated constraint), and it costs at most a third of a point on the historical markets. The ochre dashed line repeats it with the collection choice removed entirely: the certified exact minimum of β over every jointly affordable collection, computed as described just below.
  5. The dark-blue dashed line: the ceiling one model already forces. For each member alone, apply its best fixed relabeling (chosen with hindsight) and count the errors that remain; call the smallest such error, over the collection's members, the best member's decoded error. No common core can be larger than twice that number: the core must hold that member at fifty percent, so at least half the core's weight must sit on that member's remaining errors, and there are only so many of them. The dark-blue dashed line plots this ceiling. It needs no optimisation, only counting, and is computed independently of step 4. The red dashed line is the same ceiling with no relabeling allowed (twice the best member's raw error), and the violet line is the share of questions every member answers wrong at once.
  6. ★Read the chart by comparing the lines. If the market really leaves no arbitrage at B, light blue must reach black. Where it falls short (the orange shading), the shortfall is a certificate: the proof behind step 4 is constructive, so a gap of d guarantees that some fixed lookup-table combination of the collection beats the best affordable single model by at least d/2 accuracy points on these questions. In the widgets, the certified combination itself is then extracted from the linear program's dual and shown: its explicit affine weights over the collection, the decision rule, and its verified error, starred on the chart at its support cost. And where light blue sits below the dark-blue ceiling, the members are certified as genuinely complementary; where the two coincide, the test certifies no complementarity at all. Finally, the orange line plots twice the error of the best combination the search actually finds at each budget (every model raw and relabeled, plus plurality votes over affordable subsets: exhaustive over every subset on small menus and over every subset of up to five near-frontier models, with sampled larger supports elsewhere), so the distance between black and orange is twice the found arbitrage; the magenta line plots that difference directly. The certified combinations from both the greedy and the minimizing collections are folded into this line; even so it remains a lower bound on the true arbitrage, because the exact best weighted vote under a support-cost constraint is a combinatorial optimization no search here solves. Orange can sit below black even where light blue reaches black: arbitrage without a certificate, the one-directionality made visible.

In one line, the sandwich the curves trace out:

2 × (best combination's error on G)  ≤  β  ≤  2 × (best single member's decoded error)

with the prediction being β ≥ 2α(B) wherever the market is efficient at B. One caution before the charts: the certificate is one-directional. Light blue below black proves a frontier-beating combination exists; light blue at or above black proves nothing either way.

One more line removes the last judgment call. The light-blue core belongs to one constructed collection, a choice among exponentially many. The ochre dashed line is the certified exact minimum of β over all jointly affordable collections, computed without exhaustive search: since adding a model can only shrink a core, the core of the union of every individually affordable model bounds the minimum from below and any affordable candidate bounds it from above; when the two meet, two LPs certify the budget, and an exact branch-and-bound settles the rest. Every budget on this page is certified. In the real markets the exact minimum always equals the best single affordable model's relabeled core; in the synthetic market it finds the trio the moment the three are jointly affordable.

Why the light-blue line never dips below the dark-blue one

Clicking around the markets above, one regularity stands out: the light-blue core always sits exactly on the dark-blue ceiling, never below it. That is not an artifact of the computation. A ceiling gap is a demanding condition. To reach the ceiling, the program must bring the collection's best member exactly to chance: take all of its errors plus an equal mass of questions it answers correctly, chosen so that every other member is held at half too. Against any single other member this is always possible, because every other member has at least as many errors as the best one. The construction can only fail jointly, when several members would each need that balancing set to consist mostly of their own errors at once, which requires their errors to be nearly disjoint and their accuracies nearly equal. A gap below the ceiling is certified teamwork, and teamwork needs error diversity.

Real model menus have almost none: models make the same mistakes. In the January 2025 collection, between 63 and 82 percent of the best member's errors are shared with each individual other member, three to eight times what independent errors would give. Because everyone fails on largely the same questions, one balancing set serves all members at once. A counterfactual proves this is the mechanism: keep every member's exact error rate, but scatter its errors uniformly at random, and re-run the identical computation.

Ranges are over three random seeds. With independent mistakes the 2025 core collapses from a quarter of the benchmark to three percent; a market like that would also be wildly inefficient, with simple votes demolishing the frontier, which is why the hypothesis predicts you will never observe one. So the two dials that create a ceiling gap are low error overlap and near-equal accuracies. The market below turns both, on purpose.

A market built to violate the hypothesis

Every market above is real, and the test never certified any collective advantage: the light-blue line never once dipped below the dark-blue ceiling. A fair worry is that the test is simply blunt. So here is a fake market where a collective advantage exists by construction. Three thousand synthetic questions carry a difficulty rank. Five plausible models have nested errors, the way real menus do: tiny ($0.10 per thousand queries, error 0.46), value ($0.45, 0.295), workhorse ($4, 0.22), premium ($20, 0.15) and flagship ($60, 0.11). Then three specialists at $0.50 each, every one of them error 0.30 and strictly dominated by value, which is cheaper and better: individually they are worthless. But their errors are carved deliberately: all three fail on the shared hardest 8%, and each fails on its own private 22% slice that the other two get right. A majority vote of the three is wrong only on the shared 8%: error 0.080 for $1.50 per thousand, beating every model on the menu, including the flagship at forty times the price.

The widget shows what the test does with it. Once all three specialists fit inside the collection (from $3 per thousand on), the light-blue line collapses to 0.32, far below both the black line and the dark-blue ceiling at 0.59. That gap between light and dark blue, 27 points of collective margin, is the picture that appeared nowhere in the ten real markets: the test certifying that members genuinely cover for one another. The certified-combination card shows the extracted rule, equal one-third weights on three members, and the star sits far beneath every model on the menu. The orange line makes the inefficiency itself visible: from $1.50 per thousand on it sits at 2 × 0.080 = 0.16, below the black line at every budget, including the budgets where nothing is certified. Two honest wrinkles are visible too. At $1.53 the three specialists are jointly affordable, yet nothing fires, because the cheapest-first membership rule fills tiny and value before a second specialist fits (the ochre exact-minimum line has no such blind spot: it drops to 0.32 right at $1.53, with the trio itself as the certified minimizing collection); the miss belongs to the collection rule, not the test. And from $46 on, where premium and flagship pull the frontier down to meet 0.32, the deficit returns to zero even though the vote still beats the frontier (0.080 against 0.110): the certificate is one-directional, exactly as stated above.

What to take away

Across ten real markets and 384 collections of jointly affordable models, the hard core always exists, is always exactly as large as the theory demands, and is always exactly as large as the best single member allows: the models' abilities are nested, not complementary. The only combinations the test could ever certify were fixes to one model's mishandled answers, and the market absorbed those by 2025, after which the no-arbitrage condition binds to a hundredth of a point. When a market is built so that combining genuinely pays, the same computation says so immediately, names the combination, and prices its advantage. On this benchmark, for fixed combinations of models, the efficient market hypothesis for AI is not just consistent with the data; it binds with equality.

Method: the exact hard-core linear programs of the working draft Ensemble Arbitrage and the Efficient Market Hypothesis for AI (Propositions 3.1 and 6.1), solved with HiGHS; certified combinations extracted from the dual and verified by direct counting. Data: HELM's public per-question MMLU leaderboard predictions (four snapshots, 79 models, 14,038 questions) and the project's own API collections (six snapshots, 81 models, 250 questions), each at its snapshot date's list prices.
