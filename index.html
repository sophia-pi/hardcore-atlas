The questions every affordable AI model fails together

If the market for AI models is efficient, the cheap models must share one common set of hopeless questions: a hard core. This page computes that set exactly, in ten model markets from 2023 to 2026, lets you click through every market and budget to see what the computation found, and shows what the same test does to a market deliberately built to violate the theory.

September 2026 · from the efficient-AI-markets project · benchmark: MMLU

The prediction being tested

Language models are sold as API calls at prices spanning two orders of magnitude, and the expensive ones are usually more accurate. The efficient market hypothesis for AI says that in a competitive market, no cheap trick that combines the models already for sale should beat the best single model you could buy for the same money; if one existed, someone would sell it, and the advantage would disappear into the price list.

That hypothesis has a sharp, testable consequence. Take any collection of models a buyer could call together within a budget. If no fixed combination of them beats the best single affordable model, then they must share a hard core: one set of questions, covering at least twice the best model's error rate, on which every model in the collection does no better than a coin flip, even after the best fixed relabeling of its answers. Not merely "they all score poorly": any reliable edge on those questions, in either direction, is something a weighted vote could harvest. Five 90-percent models with no vote that beats them must all be right on the same 80 percent of the test. The size and existence of that core is computable from the models' recorded answers, which is what this page does.

How each line was computed, step by step

Everything on this page comes from one procedure, repeated per market and per budget. In the charts below, the horizontal axis is the buyer's budget, and each line is drawn in the margin beside the step that computes it. Here is exactly what was done.

  1. Rebuild the market as it stood on the date. Take every model released and on sale at the snapshot date, at that date's list prices, and compute its cost per query. Take each model's recorded answer to every question: 14,038 MMLU questions from HELM's public per-question predictions for the four historical markets, and 2,000 MMLU questions from our own API calls for the six live markets. An answer is one of the letters A to D, or "no parse" when the model's output contained no readable letter. We keep "no parse" as a fifth output symbol rather than discarding it; that choice matters later.
  2. The black line: twice the frontier error. Lay a grid of budgets over the market's price range. At each budget B, the frontier is the most accurate single model whose per-query price is at most B; call its error rate α(B). The black line plots 2α(B). It is doubled because that is the size the theorem demands of the core: if no combination beats the frontier, a hard core of size at least 2α(B) must exist. (Why twice: the core must hold each model at fifty percent, so about half of it falls on questions the model gets right, and only the other half on its α of errors.)
  3. ▲▲Pick the collections to test. The theorem binds every jointly affordable collection at once, and the ochre line of the next step minimizes over all of them. For per-collection detail the pages also carry one concrete worked example, hidden behind the legend toggles by default: at each budget we form the near-frontier affordable collection: every model whose error is within one point of the best model at or below its own price (so, models that are honest value at their price, not junk), added cheapest first for as long as the collection's combined price still fits within B. This is a set of models one buyer could call together on the same query, for at most B. When you select a budget in the widget, these are the triangles, each drawn at its own price and twice its error.
  4. The hard core of a collection; the ochre, green and light-blue lines. Now the actual measurement. We look for a weighting of the benchmark: each question i gets a weight wi between 0 and 1 (so no question can be counted more than once, which forces the core to be spread out rather than piled on a few freak questions). The weighting must satisfy, for every model in the collection and for every fixed relabeling of its five output symbols into answer letters (all 45 = 1,024 maps per model, such as "read C as B, read no-parse as A"): under these weights, the model answers at most half of the weight correctly. The same must hold for each constant guess, so no answer letter may carry more than half the total weight. Among all weightings satisfying this, a linear program finds one of maximum total weight, exactly; that maximum, as a share of the benchmark, is the collection's hard core size β. (A larger total weight also means a more spread-out core once normalised, so "largest" and "smoothest" are the same thing.) Three lines come from this quantity. The ochre dashed line is the headline: the certified exact minimum of β over every jointly affordable collection, computed by the union-and-candidate search unfolded below, so no collection choice is left in it. The green dotted line is the same minimum with one extra restriction: every question must be fully in or fully out of the core (weights exactly 0 or 1), so the core is a plain subset of questions. That restriction turns each linear program into an integer program; it is still solved exactly, by a branch-and-bound over the same constraints, and the same budget-by-budget certification applies. It comes out equal to the ochre minimum rounded to whole questions in every market, so the green dots lie exactly on the ochre dashes: making the core a plain list of questions costs nothing. The light-blue line is β for the worked example of step 3 alone; it is hidden by default.
  5. The ceilings one model forces, and the no-relabeling minimum. For each member alone, apply its best fixed relabeling (chosen with hindsight) and count the errors that remain; the smallest such error over the collection's members is the best member's decoded error. No common core can be larger than twice that number: the core must hold that member at fifty percent, so at least half of the core's weight must sit on that member's remaining errors, and there are only so many of them. The dark-blue dashed line plots this ceiling for the worked example; it needs no optimisation, only counting, and is computed independently of step 4. The red dashed line is the same ceiling with no relabeling allowed: every model read exactly as written, an answer that did not parse counted as wrong (twice the best member's raw error). The brick-red dotted line takes that no-relabeling rule back to step 4 and minimizes: each collection's core now carries one requirement per member instead of 1,024 (fewer requirements, so more weightings qualify and the core can only grow), and the minimum over all jointly affordable collections is certified budget by budget like the ochre line, each step one exact linear program. In every real market this minimum lands exactly on the black line at every budget: once relabeling is forbidden, no affordable collection of any composition can be pushed below twice the frontier error, the cleanest statement that every certified arbitrage was an answer-format fix. The designed market is the exception: its minimum drops to 0.32 with the specialist trio, whose advantage is a genuine vote and never needed relabeling. Last, the violet line is the share of the benchmark on which every individually affordable model (every model on the menu costing at most B, regardless of any collection) is wrong at once; it steps down as a growing budget admits more models, and no combination of affordable models can be right on those questions.
  6. ★Read the chart by comparing the lines. If the market really leaves no arbitrage at B, the ochre minimum must reach black, and with it every collection's β. Where ochre falls short (the orange shading), the shortfall is a certificate: the proof behind step 4 is constructive, so a gap of d guarantees that some fixed lookup-table combination of the minimizing collection beats the best affordable single model by at least d/2 accuracy points on these questions. The certified combination itself is extracted from the linear program's dual and shown in the sidebar: explicit affine weights over the collection, the decision rule, and its verified error, starred on the chart at its support cost. (With the worked example toggled on: where its light-blue core sits below its dark-blue ceiling, its members are certified as genuinely complementary; where the two coincide, the test certifies no complementarity at all.) The orange line is a search, not a certificate: twice the error of the best combination actually found at each budget, over every model raw and relabeled plus plurality votes over affordable subsets (exhaustive over every subset on small menus and over every subset of up to five near-frontier models, with sampled larger supports elsewhere; the certified combinations are folded in). The distance between black and orange is twice the found arbitrage, and the solid magenta line plots that difference directly. The dotted magenta line repeats it with relabeling forbidden: raw single models and plurality votes only, an unparsed answer counted as wrong; where solid magenta is positive but dotted magenta sits at zero, everything the search found was an answer-format fix. Both gaps are also painted as full-height column washes: solid magenta wherever the found gap is positive, striped magenta wherever it survives with relabeling forbidden, darker for larger gaps, so the arbitrage budgets can be spotted without reading the lines. Both are measured in hindsight on the same questions the search used, and both are lower bounds on the true arbitrage, because the exact best weighted vote under a support-cost constraint is a combinatorial optimization no search here solves. Orange can sit below black even where the ochre minimum reaches black: arbitrage without a certificate, the one-directionality made visible.

In one line, the sandwich the curves trace out:

2 × (best combination's error on G)  ≤  β  ≤  2 × (best single member's decoded error)

with the prediction being β ≥ 2α(B) for every affordable collection wherever the market is efficient; equivalently, the ochre minimum must stay at or above black. One caution before the charts: the certificate is one-directional. Ochre below black proves a frontier-beating combination exists; ochre at or above black proves nothing either way.

One more line removes the last judgment call. The light-blue core belongs to one constructed collection, a choice among exponentially many. The ochre dashed line is the certified exact minimum of β over all jointly affordable collections, computed without exhaustive search: since adding a model can only shrink a core, the core of the union of every individually affordable model bounds the minimum from below and any affordable candidate bounds it from above; when the two meet, two LPs certify the budget, and an exact branch-and-bound settles the rest. Every budget on this page is certified. In the real markets the exact minimum always equals the best single affordable model's relabeled core; in the synthetic market it finds the trio the moment the three are jointly affordable.

How the ochre line is computed: the search, unfolded

The target is m(B): the minimum of β over every collection of models whose combined price fits in B. There are exponentially many collections, so the search leans on one fact:

adding a model can only shrink a core:   G₁ ⊆ G₂  ⇒  β(G₁) ≥ β(G₂)

Phase 1 · two LPs, no search

  1. Lower bound. Solve β for the union of every individually affordable model, even though that union usually costs far more than B. Every affordable collection is a subset of it, so by the fact above none can have a smaller core.
  2. Upper bound. Solve β for one affordable candidate collection, the seed. The seed starts as the previous budget's winning collection (still affordable, since budgets only grow); then every affordable one-model collection is checked, and if any single model alone has a smaller core, that singleton replaces the seed. Any affordable example's β is an upper bound on the minimum, so the seed is simply the best example produced without searching; in these markets the best relabeled single model usually is the true minimizer, which is why this cheap candidate so often matches the lower bound exactly.
  3. Match? If the two values agree, the minimum is certified, with no subset ever enumerated. In every real market, at every budget, they agreed; January 2025 took 118 LPs against its 277 possible collections.

Phase 2 · exact branch and bound (only the synthetic market ever needed it)

  1. A node is a pair of sets: models committed in (S) and models still undecided (R); all others are out. The node stands for every affordable collection between S and S ∪ R.
  2. Trim. Drop from R any model too expensive to fit beside S.
  3. Bound. Solve β(S ∪ R). By the fact above, it lower-bounds every collection the node represents.
  4. Prune. If that bound is at least the best value found so far, nothing in the node can improve the answer; discard the node whole.
  5. Close. If S ∪ R is itself affordable, it is the node's best collection; record it as the new best and stop descending.
  6. Branch. Otherwise pick the most expensive undecided model and search both worlds: with it committed in, and with it ruled out.

Every collection lives in exactly one explored or pruned node, and pruning only discards provably non-improving ones, so the result is exact. Speed comes from reuse that never changes the answer: one global cache of solved β values; budgets swept smallest to largest with the previous winner as the seed; nodes whose union already fit inside the previous certified budget skipped; and a cap on LP calls that would downgrade a budget's answer to a reported interval rather than overclaim (it never triggered). The search logic was validated against brute-force enumeration, including a stress case where the winning collection at a larger budget excludes the smaller budget's winner.

Why the light-blue line never dips below the dark-blue one

Clicking around the markets above, one regularity stands out: the ochre minimum always equals the best affordable model's relabeled core, and, with the greedy layers toggled on, every collection's light-blue core sits exactly on its dark-blue ceiling, never below it. That is not an artifact of the computation. A ceiling gap is a demanding condition. To reach the ceiling, the program must bring the collection's best member exactly to chance: take all of its errors plus an equal mass of questions it answers correctly, chosen so that every other member is held at half too. Against any single other member this is always possible, because every other member has at least as many errors as the best one. The construction can only fail jointly, when several members would each need that balancing set to consist mostly of their own errors at once, which requires their errors to be nearly disjoint and their accuracies nearly equal. A gap below the ceiling is certified teamwork, and teamwork needs error diversity.

Real model menus have almost none: models make the same mistakes. In the January 2025 collection, between 63 and 82 percent of the best member's errors are shared with each individual other member, three to eight times what independent errors would give. Because everyone fails on largely the same questions, one balancing set serves all members at once. A counterfactual proves this is the mechanism: keep every member's exact error rate, but scatter its errors uniformly at random, and re-run the identical computation.

Ranges are over three random seeds. With independent mistakes the 2025 core collapses from a quarter of the benchmark to three percent; a market like that would also be wildly inefficient, with simple votes demolishing the frontier, which is why the hypothesis predicts you will never observe one. So the two dials that create a ceiling gap are low error overlap and near-equal accuracies. The market below turns both, on purpose.

Why the search finds combinations the test never certifies

Two facts from the charts sit together oddly. In most markets there are budgets where the orange line is below the black one: the search finds a combination, often a genuine vote over several models rather than a relabeling of one, that makes fewer errors on the benchmark than the frontier model does. Yet the ochre line, the certificate, only ever dips below black where the winning trick is a relabeling of a single model's outputs. The test and the search look at the same models answering the same questions. Why does one see arbitrage the other never does?

Because they ask different questions. The search asks: counted over all 14,038 questions, does the combination make fewer errors than the frontier model? The core asks something much harder. Recall how the core is computed: the program may put more weight on some questions and less on others, and the core is large if there is some way of weighting the questions under which every member of the collection does no better than a coin flip. Turned around, a combination only shrinks the core, and so only produces a certificate, if it stays better than chance under every weighting the program could pick, including the most hostile one. The most hostile weighting is easy to describe: put the weight on the questions the frontier model itself gets wrong. If the combination is no better than a blind guess on those questions, the program piles the weight exactly there, every member is held at a coin flip, and the core stays at full size no matter how well the combination does everywhere else.

So the question becomes concrete: on which questions do the found combinations actually win? Split the benchmark in two, the questions the frontier model gets wrong and the questions it gets right, and account for the combination's advantage across the two halves. In January 2025 at $5.62 per 1,000 queries, for example, the frontier is claude-3-5-sonnet, wrong on 1,735 of the 14,038 questions (12.4 percent), and the search finds a five-model vote beating it by 0.56 points:

Read the last column first. On the questions the frontier model gets wrong, the real markets' found votes are right about 20 percent of the time, less than the 25 percent a blind guess among four choices would score (on 39 percent of those questions, every one of the five members is wrong at once). Their half-point advantage is a bookkeeping surplus over the whole benchmark: they fix a modest number of the frontier's mistakes and give most of that back as new mistakes on questions the frontier had right. An advantage of that shape has no defense against a hostile weighting. Concentrate the weight on the frontier's errors and the vote, like each of its members, sits at guessing level; the core stays at its ceiling; nothing is certified. The designed market's trio has the opposite shape: right on 73 percent of the questions its frontier gets wrong, at no cost elsewhere. There is no weighting of questions under which that trio is held to a coin flip, so its core collapses and the certificate fires.

This also sharpens what “the models' skills are nested” means on this page. Taken literally, nesting would say: every question the frontier model gets wrong, every cheaper model also gets wrong. If that were exactly true, no combination could ever beat the frontier and the orange line would never sit below black at all. Real markets are not quite that: on the questions the frontier gets right, the other models make scattered, partly different mistakes, and that disagreement is what the found votes harvest, worth half a point to a point and a half when the combination is picked in hindsight. But on the questions the frontier gets wrong, the mistakes really are close to nested: the members are mostly wrong together, and no vote among them beats blind guessing. The core test asks its question exactly on those questions, which is why it certifies the relabeling fixes (a relabeling does help on the frontier's own errors: the model knew the answer and mangled the format) and never the votes. The decorrelation counterfactual above is the same fact run backwards: keep every model's error rate, scatter which questions each one misses at random, and votes beat guessing on the frontier's errors easily; the certificates the test would then issue are worth 12 to 21 points.

Two cautions. The found combinations are scored on the same questions the search used to choose them, so part of their small advantage is selection luck; the main audit's cross-fitted numbers are the fair judge of what survives on fresh questions. And some of the questions every model gets wrong are plausibly mislabeled or genuinely ambiguous; no combination of models can fix those, and they prop up every core. A market built to violate the hypothesis

Every market above is real, and the test never certified any collective advantage: the light-blue line never once dipped below the dark-blue ceiling. A fair worry is that the test is simply blunt. So here is a fake market where a collective advantage exists by construction. Three thousand synthetic questions carry a difficulty rank. Five plausible models have nested errors, the way real menus do: tiny ($0.10 per thousand queries, error 0.46), value ($0.45, 0.295), workhorse ($4, 0.22), premium ($20, 0.15) and flagship ($60, 0.11). Then three specialists at $0.50 each, every one of them error 0.30 and strictly dominated by value, which is cheaper and better: individually they are worthless. But their errors are carved deliberately: all three fail on the shared hardest 8%, and each fails on its own private 22% slice that the other two get right. A majority vote of the three is wrong only on the shared 8%: error 0.080 for $1.50 per thousand, beating every model on the menu, including the flagship at forty times the price.

The widget shows what the test does with it. The moment the trio is jointly affordable, $1.53 per thousand, the ochre minimum collapses to 0.32, far below the black line at 0.59. Toggle the greedy layers on and the per-collection picture appears too: from $3 the light-blue line falls 27 points below its dark-blue ceiling, the certified collective margin that showed up nowhere in the ten real markets, the test certifying that members genuinely cover for one another. The certified-combination card shows the extracted rule, equal one-third weights on three members, and the star sits far beneath every model on the menu. The orange line makes the inefficiency itself visible: from $1.50 per thousand on it sits at 2 × 0.080 = 0.16, below the black line at every budget, including the budgets where nothing is certified. Two honest wrinkles are visible too. The worked example's light-blue layer only collapses from $3, because its cheapest-first fill spends the budget on tiny and value before a second specialist fits; that blind spot belongs to the example's membership rule, and the ochre minimum does not share it. And from $46 on, where premium and flagship pull the frontier down to meet 0.32, the deficit returns to zero even though the vote still beats the frontier (0.080 against 0.110): the certificate is one-directional, exactly as stated above.

Does the price convention matter? A tokenizer check

Every dollar figure above rests on one assumption. Providers price tokens, not questions, so each model's per-query cost is its list price applied to a nominal query of 700 input tokens (the 5-shot prompt) plus one output token, the same for every model. Real tokenizers disagree about how long the same text is, and cost enters this experiment only through comparisons: which model is the most accurate you can afford, which collections are jointly affordable, where the certified gaps sit. A per-model error in token counts could in principle flip any of those at a budget boundary. So it was measured.

The 5-shot MMLU prompt was re-tokenized under seventeen real tokenizers: OpenAI's two (exact, via tiktoken) and the published tokenizer files of the Llama, Mistral, Qwen, Yi, DeepSeek, Phi, Gemma, OLMo, Jamba, Solar and Arctic families. Vendors with no public tokenizer at all (Anthropic, Google's API models, Amazon Nova, Cohere) were swept across the whole range of the measured ones, moved together, as a worst case. Two consequences, one cosmetic and one structural. Cosmetically, the nominal 700 overstates every model's cost by roughly 10 to 25 percent, so the dollar labels throughout are a little high; a uniform overestimate slides every chart along its log axis and changes no comparison. The structural question is the spread between models, about plus or minus nine percent, and the scale-free comparison answers it. With per-model costs rebuilt from the measured counts, October 2023 and January 2025 are identical in the frontier sequence, the certified-gap pairs and the greedy-collection sequence, even with the unmeasured vendors pushed to either extreme of the bracket. In April and July 2024 a few near-tied cheap models trade brief frontier segments (qwen1.5-7b picks one up; gemma-7b's and llama-3-8b's short segments pass to phi-3-small), and the worked example's membership shifts at one or two budgets, which the ochre minimum, defined over all collections at once, does not care about. Every certified gap in every market remains the same single model's relabel fix. And the core sizes and ceilings involve no prices at all, so they cannot move.

What to take away

Across ten real markets and 384 collections of jointly affordable models, the hard core always exists, is always exactly as large as the theory demands, and is always exactly as large as the best single member allows: the models' abilities are nested, not complementary. The only combinations the test could ever certify were fixes to one model's mishandled answers, and the market absorbed those by 2025, after which the no-arbitrage condition binds to a hundredth of a point. When a market is built so that combining genuinely pays, the same computation says so immediately, names the combination, and prices its advantage. On this benchmark, for fixed combinations of models, the efficient market hypothesis for AI is not just consistent with the data; it binds with equality.
